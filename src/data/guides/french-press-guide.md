## Why French press

French press is the most forgiving way to make genuinely good coffee at home. There's no pouring technique to learn, no paper filter to buy, and the full-immersion method is naturally even — the coffee steeps in the water the way tea does, so extraction is hard to mess up badly. What you get is a full-bodied, rich cup with more oils and texture than any filtered method. What you trade away is clarity: there will always be a little sediment, and the flavors run together more than in a pour-over.

It's also the cheapest entry point. A press, a scale, and a grinder that can do coarse — that's the whole kit. [AFFILIATE:french-press]

## The recipe

For a standard 34-oz (1-liter) press:

- **Coffee:** 30g, ground coarse (breadcrumb texture)
- **Water:** 420g at about 200°F / 93°C — just off the boil
- **Ratio:** 1:14, adjustable from 1:12 (stronger) to 1:15 (lighter)
- **Time:** 4 minutes

Scale it down for smaller presses — the ratio is what matters, not the absolute numbers.

## Step by step

1. **Heat and dose.** Bring water to a boil. Add 30g of coarse-ground coffee to the empty press. (Some people preheat the beaker with hot water and dump it — nice touch, optional.)
2. **Pour and start the timer.** Pour all 420g of water over the grounds, making sure everything gets wet. Give it one gentle stir with a spoon — non-metal, so you don't scratch the glass — to break up any dry clumps. Start your 4-minute timer.
3. **Wait.** Put the lid on (plunger pulled all the way up) to keep heat in. Let it steep, undisturbed.
4. **Break the crust.** At 4 minutes, you'll see a crust of grounds floating on top. Take a spoon and gently break through it, stirring lightly. A lot of foam and floating bits will come up.
5. **Skim.** Spoon off the foam and floating grounds from the surface. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that separates a clean French press from a muddy one. Those floaters are bitter and silty — get them out.
6. **Plunge — gently.** Place the plunger in and press down slowly and evenly, stopping just above the coffee bed. Don't jam it to the bottom; that squeezes bitterness out of the grounds.
7. **Serve immediately.** Pour all the coffee out right away — into cups or a carafe. Coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter within minutes. This is the most common French press mistake there is.

## Cleanup

Don't pour grounds down the sink — they settle in pipes and cause clogs over time. Scoop or pour them into the trash or compost (gardeners love them). Rinse the beaker and plunger with hot water. Every few brews, disassemble the plunger — unscrew the mesh screens and wash them properly. Coffee oils build up on the mesh and go rancid, and rancid oil is what makes a neglected press produce cups that taste stale no matter how fresh the beans are.

## Common mistakes

- **Grind too fine.** The #1 issue. Fine grounds over-extract in 4 minutes and slip through the mesh as sludge. When in doubt, go coarser.
- **Leaving it to sit after plunging.** The press is not a thermos. Decant everything immediately.
- **Skipping the skim.** Breaking the crust and skimming takes 30 seconds and removes the bitterest, siltiest part of the brew.
- **Stale beans.** Immersion is forgiving, but it can't resurrect coffee that's been open for two months. Buy whole bean in quantities you'll finish in two to three weeks.
- **Eyeballing the dose.** "A few scoops" varies wildly. The scale is non-negotiable — it's the cheapest upgrade in all of coffee.

French press won't give you the sparkling clarity of a pour-over or the intensity of espresso, and it doesn't try to. It's the reliable workhorse: rich, simple, and hard to get wrong once the grind and the decanting are right.
