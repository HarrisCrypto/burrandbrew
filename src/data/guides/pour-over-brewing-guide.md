## What you need (and what you can skip)

Pour-over has a reputation for gear obsession, but the required kit is short:

- **A dripper.** The cone or flat-bottomed funnel the coffee sits in. Cones (like the classic V-shape) emphasize clarity; flat-bottom drippers are more forgiving of pour technique. Either makes great coffee. [AFFILIATE:pour-over-dripper]
- **Paper filters** sized for your dripper. Rinse them before brewing.
- **A kettle.** A gooseneck kettle gives you control over where the water goes and how fast. [AFFILIATE:gooseneck-kettle] A regular kettle works — your pours will just be less precise, which matters more than it sounds.
- **A scale accurate to 0.1g.** This is the piece beginners skip and shouldn't. [AFFILIATE:coffee-scale] Pour-over ratios are by weight, and the difference between 1:15 and 1:17 is the difference between a great cup and a forgettable one. You can't eyeball it.
- **A burr grinder.** Fresh, evenly ground coffee ground just before brewing. Pre-ground coffee goes stale fast and is rarely ground correctly for pour-over.

What you can skip at the start: a dedicated pouring kettle stand, a thermometer (boil and wait ~30 seconds, or just boil), and any gadget that promises to automate the pour. Learn the manual technique first; you'll taste when it matters.

## The ratio: your starting point

Weigh everything. A good starting ratio is **1:16** — for example, 20g of coffee to 320g of water. The accepted working range is **1:15 to 1:17**.

- **1:15** — stronger, more intense. Good if your coffee tastes thin or hollow.
- **1:16** — the balanced middle. Start here.
- **1:17** — lighter, more delicate. Good if your coffee tastes heavy or muddy.

Pick one ratio, lock it in, and change grind or technique first. Ratio is the last thing to move, not the first.

## Grind size and water temperature

- **Grind:** medium-fine — roughly the texture of sea salt, maybe a touch finer. If your brews consistently finish much faster or slower than expected, grind is the first suspect.
- **Water:** **195–205°F (90–96°C)**. Just off the boil is fine for most coffees. Very light roasts sometimes do better at the top of that range; darker roasts can taste cleaner a touch cooler. But treat temperature as a fine-tuning lever — fix grind and pour technique before you start fiddling with degrees.

## The technique, step by step

Total brew time for a 20g dose is typically around 2:30–3:30. Here's the sequence:

1. **Rinse.** Place the filter in the dripper, set it on your vessel, and pour hot water through the filter. This washes out papery taste and preheats everything. Dump the rinse water — don't let it sit in the vessel.
2. **Dose and settle.** Add your ground coffee (20g, say). Give the dripper a gentle shake to level the bed.
3. **Bloom.** Start your timer. Pour just enough water to saturate all the grounds — about twice the coffee's weight (40–50g for a 20g dose). Fresh coffee will bubble and swell as CO₂ escapes. Wait 30–45 seconds. If the coffee doesn't bloom much, it's probably not very fresh — still drinkable, just less lively.
4. **The main pours.** Pour in slow, steady spirals from the center outward, avoiding pouring directly on the filter walls. A common pattern: pour to ~200g, wait for the water to draw down a bit, then pour to your final 320g. Keep the water level fairly consistent rather than dumping it all at once — gentle agitation extracts evenly; a flood followed by a long stall does not.
5. **Let it draw down.** Once you've hit your total water weight, let the coffee finish dripping through. The bed should look flat and even at the end. A dome in the middle or grounds stuck high on the filter walls means your pouring was uneven — aim more carefully next time.

## Troubleshooting: sour vs bitter

Almost every bad pour-over is one of two things:

- **Sour, sharp, salty, thin** — under-extracted. The water didn't pull enough out of the coffee. Fix it by grinding finer, pouring a little slower (more contact time), or using hotter water.
- **Bitter, dry, harsh, ashy** — over-extracted. The water pulled too much out. Fix it by grinding coarser, pouring a little faster, or using slightly cooler water.
- **Hollow or watery but not exactly sour** — try a stronger ratio (1:15) before touching the grind.
- **Muddy or heavy but not exactly bitter** — try a weaker ratio (1:17).

Golden rule: **change one variable at a time**, and give each change two or three brews before judging. Coffee varies bag to bag, and your palate needs a couple of cups to calibrate.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **No scale.** The single biggest one. "Two scoops" is not a measurement.
- **Water straight down the middle only.** The grounds at the edges never get properly extracted. Spiral outward.
- **Pouring on the filter paper.** Water running down the paper bypasses the coffee entirely — weak, thin cups.
- **Grinding the night before.** Ground coffee stales in hours, not days. Grind right before you brew.
- **Changing everything at once.** New grind, new ratio, new temperature, new pour pattern — and now you have no idea what fixed or broke the cup.

Pour-over rewards attention more than equipment. A basic dripper, fresh beans, a scale, and a careful pour will beat an expensive setup brewed carelessly every single time.
