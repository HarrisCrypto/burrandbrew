## What espresso is

Espresso is coffee brewed under pressure. Hot water — around 200°F / 93°C — is forced through a puck of finely ground coffee at roughly 9 bars of pressure, producing a small, concentrated shot in about 25 to 30 seconds. The pressure does two things other methods can't: it extracts a lot of flavor very fast, and it emulsifies oils and CO₂ into crema, the reddish-brown foam that caps a fresh shot.

That concentration is why espresso is so sensitive. A pour-over has minutes of contact time and a forgiving ratio; espresso has half a minute and nowhere to hide. Small changes — a gram of dose, a few seconds of time — show up immediately in the cup. That's the challenge and the appeal.

## The three variables: dose, yield, time

Every espresso recipe is built from three numbers:

- **Dose:** the weight of dry ground coffee going in. Common home doses are 16–20g; 18g is the standard starting point for a double basket.
- **Yield:** the weight of liquid espresso coming out. Weigh it, don't measure volume — crema makes volume misleading.
- **Time:** how long the shot takes, measured from when you start the pump (or from first drip, if your machine pre-infuses — just be consistent about which).

These three relate through the **brew ratio** — yield divided by dose. The classic starting point is **1:2**: 18g in, 36g out, in **25–30 seconds**. That ratio has a name for a reason: it tends to land in the balanced zone for most coffees.

The ratio is also your flavor lever:

- **Ristretto (~1:1 to 1:1.5):** shorter shot, less water through the puck. Sweeter, more intense, heavier body — but can taste underdeveloped with some coffees.
- **Normale (1:2):** the balanced middle. Start here.
- **Lungo (~1:3 and beyond):** longer shot, more water. Thinner, more bitter as extraction climbs — occasionally great with coffees that need it, often just diluted.

Time is the readout, not the target. If 18g in and 36g out takes 25–30 seconds, your grind is in the right neighborhood. If it gushes out in 15 seconds, the grind is too coarse; if it chokes and drips for 45, too fine. Adjust the grind until time and ratio land together — then stop adjusting and start tasting.

## Dialing in by taste

Here's the actual process, and it's simpler than the internet makes it look:

1. **Lock the dose.** Pick 18g (or whatever fits your basket) and don't change it. Weigh to 0.1g every time.
2. **Set a target.** 1:2 ratio in 25–30 seconds. Adjust grind until you hit it.
3. **Taste.** This is the part people skip in favor of chasing numbers. The numbers get you close; your tongue finishes the job.
4. **Adjust one thing:**
   - **Sour, sharp, salty, thin** → under-extracted. Grind finer (or extend the ratio slightly).
   - **Bitter, dry, harsh, ashy** → over-extracted. Grind coarser (or shorten the ratio slightly).
   - **Balanced but boring** → the coffee might just be that coffee. Try a different bean before blaming the technique.
5. **Repeat.** Give each change two or three shots before judging. Espresso varies shot to shot; don't chase a single outlier.

Keep notes — dose, yield, time, grind setting, and a few taste words. Memory is unreliable; a notebook isn't. After a week you'll have a personal dial-in history that makes every new bag of beans faster to figure out.

## Why consistency beats gear

The espresso forums will happily sell you the idea that the next upgrade fixes the cup. Usually it doesn't. The overwhelming majority of bad home espresso comes from inconsistency, not equipment:

- **Unweighed doses** that drift by a gram or two day to day.
- **Uneven distribution** — grounds heaped on one side, so water channels through the thin spot and the shot runs both sour and bitter at once.
- **Unlevel tamping** that does the same thing.
- **Stale beans** that no grinder can save.

Fix those and a basic setup makes genuinely good espresso. A scale accurate to 0.1g, a distribution habit (a few taps or a simple distribution tool), a level tamp, and beans roasted within the last few weeks — that's the whole foundation. This is also the right lens for upgrades. Upgrade when you've proven the habit and can name the bottleneck: the grinder can't grind fine enough, the machine's temperature swings wildly, the steam wand can't texture milk. "I want better espresso" isn't a bottleneck; "my shots channel no matter what I change" might be.

Espresso rewards the patient. Pick one coffee, learn its behavior across a week of shots, and change one thing at a time. The people making great espresso at home aren't the ones with the most expensive machines — they're the ones who weigh, taste, write it down, and do it again tomorrow.
