## What a brew ratio actually means

A brew ratio is the weight of coffee to the weight of water, written as **coffee : water**. So **1:16** means 1 gram of coffee for every 16 grams of water.

Weight matters because scoops lie. A tablespoon of light, dense beans weighs more than a tablespoon of dark, airy beans. Grams don't lie. And since 1 gram of water equals 1 milliliter, the math is painless: if your ratio is 1:16 and you use 20 grams of coffee, you need 320 grams (milliliters) of water.

This one number — more than your brewer, your kettle, or your pouring technique — determines whether your cup tastes strong or weak, thin or full. Learn it and you can repeat any great cup and fix any bad one.

## Starting ratios by method

These are the widely accepted starting points, not laws. Start here, taste, adjust.

| Method | Ratio | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Espresso | 1:2 | 18g coffee → ~36g espresso; varies with style |
| Pour-over (drip) | 1:15–1:17 | 1:16 is the classic starting point |
| French press | 1:12–1:15 | Stronger range; coarse grind |
| Cold brew concentrate | 1:8–1:10 | Dilute 1:1 with water or milk to serve |
| Moka pot | ~1:10 | Fill basket, water to the valve — ratio lands here naturally |
| AeroPress | 1:12–1:15 | Very forgiving; experiment freely |

Why does espresso use so much more coffee? Because it's a fast, pressurized extraction — the water only contacts the coffee for about 25–30 seconds, so you need a concentrated dose to get enough flavor out. Immersion methods like French press and cold brew have minutes or hours of contact time, so less coffee per unit of water still extracts plenty.

## How to use a scale

Put your brewer on the scale, tare it to zero, add coffee to your target weight. Tare again, add water to your target weight. That's the whole technique.

A basic kitchen scale that reads in 1-gram increments is fine for everything except espresso, where 0.1-gram precision genuinely helps (small doses, big flavor swings). You don't need a coffee-specific scale to start — any accurate kitchen scale works. [AFFILIATE:coffee-scale]

## A worked example

Say you want two mugs of pour-over at 1:16:

1. Decide your water: two mugs ≈ 500g of water.
2. Divide by 16: 500 ÷ 16 = 31.25g of coffee. Round to 31g.
3. Tare the scale, add 31g of ground coffee, tare, pour 500g of water.

Tastes weak? Next time try 1:15 (33g coffee for the same 500g water). Tastes harsh or muddy? Try 1:17 (29g). Change the ratio *or* the grind — not both at once — so you know what actually fixed it.

## Adjusting to taste

The ratio is a starting line, not a destination. Two people can brew the same beans at the same ratio and prefer different results. Here's how to steer:

- **Tastes weak, watery, or tea-like?** Use more coffee (move the ratio toward 1:14–1:15) or grind finer. But first check: did you actually let it brew long enough? Under-extraction tastes weak too.
- **Tastes bitter, harsh, or drying?** Use less coffee (move toward 1:16–1:17) or grind coarser. Over-extraction is the usual suspect.
- **Tastes sour or sharp?** That's usually under-extraction, not ratio — grind finer or extend the brew time before touching the ratio.
- **Tastes flat and dull?** The beans may be stale, or the water too cool. Ratio won't fix staleness.

A useful rule: adjust in small steps and keep notes. "Tuesday: 31g at 1:16, good but slightly weak → Wednesday: 33g" is how you converge on *your* ratio fast. Memory is unreliable; a scrap of paper isn't.

## Ratio vs. strength vs. extraction

Two concepts people mix up:

- **Strength** is how much coffee is in the water — controlled by the ratio. More coffee per water = stronger.
- **Extraction** is how much flavor you pulled out of the grounds — controlled by grind size, time, and temperature.

A cup can be strong *and* under-extracted (intense but sour) or weak *and* over-extracted (thin but bitter). When something tastes off, figure out which axis is wrong before you change things. Ratio fixes strength problems. Grind and time fix extraction problems.
